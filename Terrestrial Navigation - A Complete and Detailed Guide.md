# Terrestrial Navigation: A Complete and Detailed Guide

## Latitude, Longitude, Directions, Compass Bearings, Distance, Speed, Position Fixing, and Basic Navigation Principles

Terrestrial navigation is the science and practical skill of determining **where you are, where you want to go, which direction you must travel, how far you must travel, how long the journey will take, and how to determine your position along the way.**

It is used in land travel, road transportation, hiking, mountaineering, surveying, military operations, aviation, and maritime navigation near coastlines.

To navigate successfully, you must understand several interconnected concepts:

1. Terrestrial navigation and its fundamental principles.
2. The Earth and its coordinate systems.
3. Latitude and longitude.
4. Directions and the cardinal points.
5. Compass bearings and methods of measuring direction.
6. Distance and the measurement of ground distances.
7. Speed, time, and their relationship to distance.
8. Position fixing and the determination of your location.
9. Maps, scales, grids, and reference systems.
10. Practical navigation calculations and worked examples.
11. Common navigation errors and methods of preventing them.

Let's examine each topic systematically, beginning with the fundamentals.

---

# 1\. What Is Terrestrial Navigation?

## 1.1 Definition

**Terrestrial navigation is the process of determining and maintaining a person's or vehicle's position relative to the Earth's surface and selecting a suitable route to reach a desired destination.**

The term _terrestrial_ means relating to the Earth or land.

Terrestrial navigation can involve navigating across open land, following roads, travelling through forests, crossing deserts, or moving through mountainous terrain.

The basic questions that terrestrial navigation attempts to answer are:

- **Where am I?** — Determining your present position.
- **Where do I want to go?** — Identifying your destination.
- **Which direction should I travel?** — Determining the required direction or bearing.
- **How far away is the destination?** — Calculating the distance.
- **How long will it take to get there?** — Estimating the travel time.
- **How can I verify my position?** — Comparing observations with a map, landmarks, a compass, or satellite positioning.
- **Am I following the correct route?** — Monitoring progress and correcting deviations.

These questions form the foundation of practical navigation.

## 1.2 The principal elements of navigation

A navigation problem normally involves five fundamental elements.

### A. Position

Position describes the location of a person, vehicle, or object on the Earth's surface.

For example:

- A vehicle is located at a particular road intersection.
- A hiker is standing near a mountain summit.
- A surveyor is working at a specified geographical coordinate.

Position may be expressed using latitude and longitude, a map grid reference, or a clearly identified landmark.

### B. Direction

Direction describes the way in which a person or vehicle must move to reach a destination.

Examples include:

- North
- South
- East
- West
- Northeast
- Southwest

Direction can also be expressed numerically as a compass bearing, such as 045° or 270°.

### C. Distance

Distance is the amount of ground that must be covered between two points.

For example, two locations may be separated by a straight-line distance of 5 km, although the actual road journey between them might be 7 km.

### D. Speed

Speed indicates how quickly a person or vehicle moves over a distance.

It is commonly expressed in:

- Kilometres per hour (km/h)
- Metres per second (m/s)
- Miles per hour (mph)
- Knots in maritime and aviation contexts

### E. Time

Time is the duration required to travel from one position to another.

If the distance and average speed are known, the estimated travel time can be calculated.

The fundamental relationship is:

$$
\boxed{\text{Time}=\frac{\text{Distance}}{\text{Average speed}}}
$$

Together, position, direction, distance, speed, and time provide the essential information needed for basic navigation.

# 2\. The Earth and Its Shape

Understanding the Earth is essential because navigation depends on describing positions and directions on its surface.

## 2.1 The shape of the Earth

The Earth is approximately spherical, but it is not a perfect sphere.

It is slightly flattened at the poles and bulges around the equator because of its rotation.

The more accurate simplified model is an **oblate spheroid**.

For many everyday navigation problems, the Earth can be treated as a sphere. However, precise surveying and geodetic positioning use more sophisticated models.

The approximate dimensions of the Earth are:

| Property | Approximate value |
| --- | --- |
| Equatorial circumference | 40,075 km |
| Polar circumference | 40,008 km |
| Equatorial radius | 6,378 km |
| Polar radius | 6,357 km |
| Mean radius | 6,371 km |

These figures explain why geographical coordinates are necessary: a flat map cannot perfectly represent the curved surface of the entire Earth.

## 2.2 The Earth's axis

The Earth's axis is an imaginary line passing through the North Pole and South Pole.

The Earth rotates around this axis.

The two ends of the axis define the geographical poles:

- **North Pole:** 90° north latitude.
- **South Pole:** 90° south latitude.

The axis also provides the reference for determining geographical north and south.

## 2.3 The Equator

The Equator is an imaginary circle around the Earth halfway between the North Pole and the South Pole.

It divides the Earth into two hemispheres:

- **Northern Hemisphere:** The half north of the Equator.
- **Southern Hemisphere:** The half south of the Equator.

The Equator is assigned a latitude of:

$$
\boxed{0^\circ}
$$

It is the fundamental reference line from which latitude is measured.

## 2.4 Lines of latitude and longitude

The Earth's coordinate system uses two families of imaginary lines:

- **Parallels of latitude**, which run east–west.
- **Meridians of longitude**, which run from pole to pole.

These lines form a geographical coordinate grid.

The intersection of a latitude and a longitude identifies a geographical position.

For example, a point at:

$$
19^\circ \text{ N},\quad 73^\circ \text{ E}
$$

lies 19 degrees north of the Equator and 73 degrees east of the Greenwich meridian.

This pair of coordinates identifies a location in the western part of India, although more precise coordinates would be required to identify a particular street or building.

# 3\. Latitude

## 3.1 Definition of latitude

**Latitude is the angular distance of a point north or south of the Equator, measured along a meridian.**

Latitude tells us how far north or south a location lies.

It is measured from:

$$
0^\circ \text{ at the Equator}
$$

to:

$$
90^\circ \text{ at either pole}
$$

Latitude is expressed using the letters N or S.

- N means north of the Equator.
- S means south of the Equator.

Examples include:

- 10° N
- 25° N
- 40° S
- 65° S

Latitude is an angular measurement, not a direct distance measurement. However, it can be converted into an approximate surface distance.

## 3.2 Parallels of latitude

Parallels of latitude are imaginary circles running east–west around the Earth.

They are called parallels because they are parallel to the Equator.

Important characteristics include:

1. The Equator is the largest parallel.
2. Parallels become progressively smaller towards the poles.
3. Every parallel except the Equator is a smaller circle.
4. All parallels are perpendicular to the meridians in the usual geographical coordinate system.
5. Latitude determines the angular position north or south of the Equator.

The Equator divides the Earth into equal northern and southern halves.

## 3.3 Important parallels of latitude

Several parallels have special geographical significance.

| Parallel | Latitude | Significance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Equator | 0° | Divides the Northern and Southern Hemispheres |
| Tropic of Cancer | Approximately 23.44° N | Northern limit of the Sun's overhead position during the year |
| Tropic of Capricorn | Approximately 23.44° S | Southern limit of the Sun's overhead position during the year |
| Arctic Circle | Approximately 66.56° N | Southern boundary of the Arctic polar-day and polar-night region |
| Antarctic Circle | Approximately 66.56° S | Northern boundary of the Antarctic polar-day and polar-night region |
| North Pole | 90° N | Northern end of the Earth's rotational axis |
| South Pole | 90° S | Southern end of the Earth's rotational axis |

The exact astronomical positions of the tropics and polar circles vary slightly over long periods because the Earth's axial tilt changes.

## 3.4 How latitude is measured

Latitude is measured as an angle between the Equator and the location's position relative to the Earth's centre.

Imagine drawing three lines:

1. A line from the Earth's centre to a point on the Equator.
2. A line from the Earth's centre to your location.
3. The angle between these lines in the meridional plane.

That angle represents the latitude, with north or south specifying the direction from the Equator.

For ordinary navigation, you do not need to draw these lines physically. Latitude is obtained from a map, a GPS receiver, or another positioning system.

## 3.5 Degrees, minutes, and seconds

Latitude is measured in degrees, minutes, and seconds.

The relationships are:

$$
1^\circ = 60'
$$

$$
1' = 60''
$$

Therefore:

$$
1^\circ = 3600''
$$

The symbols mean:

- ° = degrees
- ′ = minutes of arc
- ″ = seconds of arc

For example:

$$
19^\circ 30' 00'' \text{ N}
$$

means 19 degrees, 30 minutes north of the Equator.

Since 30 minutes is half a degree:

$$
19^\circ 30' = 19.5^\circ
$$

Another example:

$$
19^\circ 15' 30'' \text{ N}
$$

In decimal degrees:

$$
19+\frac{15}{60}+\frac{30}{3600}
=19.258333\ldots^\circ
$$

Thus:

$$
\boxed{19^\circ 15' 30'' \text{ N}\approx19.258333^\circ \text{ N}}
$$

## 3.6 Relationship between latitude and distance

One degree of latitude corresponds to approximately 111 km of surface distance.

One minute of latitude corresponds to approximately 1.852 km.

One second of latitude corresponds to approximately 30.9 m.

The exact values vary slightly with latitude because the Earth is not a perfect sphere.

For many basic navigation calculations, the following approximations are useful:

$$
\boxed{1^\circ \text{ latitude}\approx111\text{ km}}
$$

$$
\boxed{1' \text{ latitude}\approx1.852\text{ km}}
$$

### Example 1: Calculating distance from latitude

Suppose a traveller moves from 15° N to 18° N along the same meridian.

The difference in latitude is:

$$
18^\circ-15^\circ=3^\circ
$$

Using the approximation of 111 km per degree:

$$
\text{Distance}=3\times111
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Distance}\approx333\text{ km}}
$$

This is the approximate north–south surface distance between the two points.

It does not necessarily equal the road distance.

### Example 2: Difference in latitude

A vessel or vehicle changes its latitude from 22° 10′ N to 24° 40′ N.

The difference is:

$$
24^\circ40'-22^\circ10'=2^\circ30'
$$

Convert the difference into minutes:

$$
2^\circ30'=150'
$$

Distance:

$$
150\times1.852=277.8\text{ km}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\text{North–south distance}\approx277.8\text{ km}}
$$

This calculation assumes movement along a meridian.

## 3.7 Uses of latitude in navigation

Latitude is useful for:

- Identifying geographical positions.
- Determining north–south displacement.
- Measuring approximate distances along meridians.
- Identifying climatic and geographical zones.
- Plotting locations on maps.
- Supporting route planning.
- Comparing positions obtained at different times.

**Remember:** Latitude tells you how far north or south a point lies relative to the Equator.

# 4\. Longitude

## 4.1 Definition of longitude

**Longitude is the angular distance of a point east or west of the Greenwich meridian.**

Longitude tells us how far east or west a location lies.

The reference meridian is assigned:

$$
0^\circ \text{ longitude}
$$

The longitude of a point is expressed east or west of this meridian.

- E means east longitude.
- W means west longitude.

Longitude ranges from 0° to 180° east or west.

Examples include:

- 30° E
- 75° E
- 120° W
- 150° W

The 180° meridian lies approximately opposite the Greenwich meridian.

## 4.2 The Greenwich meridian

The Greenwich meridian is the internationally adopted reference meridian for longitude.

It passes through Greenwich in London, United Kingdom.

Its longitude is:

$$
\boxed{0^\circ}
$$

It divides the globe into eastern and western longitude conventions.

The meridian was adopted as the international reference at the International Meridian Conference in 1884.

The Greenwich meridian is also associated historically with the basis of Greenwich Mean Time, although modern civil timekeeping uses UTC rather than GMT as its primary international reference.

## 4.3 Meridians of longitude

Meridians are imaginary lines connecting the North Pole and South Pole.

Their principal characteristics are:

1. They extend from pole to pole.
2. They are approximately semicircular on the globe.
3. All meridians meet at the poles.
4. They are widest apart at the Equator.
5. Their east–west separation decreases towards the poles.
6. They are used to determine longitude.

Unlike parallels of latitude, meridians are not parallel to one another.

## 4.4 Relationship between longitude and distance

The distance represented by one degree of longitude depends on latitude.

At the Equator, one degree of longitude corresponds to approximately 111.3 km.

As latitude increases, the distance between meridians decreases.

At the poles, all meridians meet, so the east–west distance between them becomes zero.

For a spherical Earth, the approximate distance corresponding to a longitude difference is:

$$
\boxed{D=111.32\cos\phi\times\Delta\lambda}
$$

where:

- $D$ = east–west distance in kilometres.
- $\phi$ = latitude.
- $\Delta\lambda$ = longitude difference in degrees.
- $\cos\phi$ = cosine of the latitude.

This approximation is suitable for relatively short east–west distances. For precise work or long distances, use an appropriate geodesic calculation.

### Example 1: Longitude distance at the Equator

Suppose two points lie at the Equator and differ by 2° of longitude.

At the Equator:

$$
\cos0^\circ=1
$$

Therefore:

$$
D=111.32\times1\times2
$$

$$
\boxed{D\approx222.64\text{ km}}
$$

### Example 2: Longitude distance at 60° latitude

Suppose two points are both at latitude 60° N and differ by 2° of longitude.

Since:

$$
\cos60^\circ=0.5
$$

Then:

$$
D=111.32\times0.5\times2
$$

$$
\boxed{D\approx111.32\text{ km}}
$$

Thus, the same longitude difference represents approximately half as much east–west distance at 60° latitude as at the Equator.

## 4.5 Calculating the difference in longitude

Consider two locations:

- Location A: 70° E
- Location B: 85° E

Their difference in longitude is:

$$
85^\circ-70^\circ=15^\circ
$$

Both are east of Greenwich, so their longitude difference is simply the difference between the values.

Now consider:

- Location A: 40° W
- Location B: 20° W

The difference is:

$$
40^\circ-20^\circ=20^\circ
$$

For locations on opposite sides of Greenwich, the differences must be handled carefully.

For example:

- Location A: 30° E
- Location B: 20° W

Their longitude difference is:

$$
30^\circ+20^\circ=50^\circ
$$

The points differ by 50° of longitude along the shorter east–west angular separation in this example.

For locations close to the 180° meridian, calculate the smaller angular separation carefully because the numerical longitude values may wrap around that meridian.

## 4.6 Longitude and time

Longitude is closely related to local solar time because the Earth rotates.

The Earth rotates through approximately:

$$
360^\circ \text{ in }24\text{ hours}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\frac{360^\circ}{24}=15^\circ\text{ per hour}
$$

So:

$$
\boxed{15^\circ \text{ longitude}\approx1\text{ hour}}
$$

And:

$$
\boxed{1^\circ \text{ longitude}\approx4\text{ minutes}}
$$

Similarly:

- 15° = 1 hour
- 30° = 2 hours
- 45° = 3 hours
- 1° = 4 minutes
- 15′ = 1 minute

### Example: Difference in local solar time

Suppose Location A is at 75° E and Location B is at 90° E.

The longitude difference is:

$$
90^\circ-75^\circ=15^\circ
$$

The corresponding local solar-time difference is:

$$
15^\circ\times4\text{ minutes}=60\text{ minutes}
$$

Therefore, the local solar time at Location B is approximately one hour ahead of that at Location A.

This relationship concerns **local solar time**, not necessarily official clock time. Time zones are political and administrative regions, and some have half-hour or quarter-hour offsets.

## 4.7 Uses of longitude in navigation

Longitude is useful for:

- Determining east–west geographical position.
- Identifying locations on maps.
- Calculating approximate east–west distances.
- Supporting route planning.
- Understanding local solar-time differences.
- Establishing geographical coordinates.
- Combining with latitude to determine a location.

**Remember:** Longitude tells you how far east or west a point lies relative to the Greenwich meridian.

# 5\. Latitude and Longitude Together

Latitude and longitude work together to define geographical position.

A single latitude identifies a parallel, while a single longitude identifies a meridian. Their intersection identifies a point on the Earth's surface.

For example:

$$
\boxed{19^\circ 04' \text{ N},\quad72^\circ 52' \text{ E}}
$$

This is an illustrative coordinate pair in the Mumbai region of India; the exact location depends on the precise values used.

The first coordinate is latitude.

The second coordinate is longitude.

The correct order is generally:

$$
\boxed{\text{Latitude first, longitude second}}
$$

## 5.1 Coordinate notation

Geographical coordinates are commonly expressed in three formats.

### A. Degrees, minutes, and seconds (DMS)

Example:

$$
19^\circ04'30''\text{ N},\quad72^\circ52'15''\text{ E}
$$

This format is useful in traditional surveying and geographical descriptions.

### B. Degrees and decimal minutes (DDM)

Example:

$$
19^\circ04.500'\text{ N},\quad72^\circ52.250'\text{ E}
$$

This format is widely used in navigation.

### C. Decimal degrees (DD)

Example:

$$
19.075000^\circ,\quad72.870833^\circ
$$

This format is commonly used in digital mapping and GPS applications.

Always check the coordinate format before entering coordinates into a navigation device.

Confusing decimal minutes with decimal degrees can place you at the wrong location.

## 5.2 Converting coordinates into decimal degrees

The formula is:

$$
\boxed{\text{Decimal degrees}
=D+\frac{M}{60}+\frac{S}{3600}}
$$

where:

- $D$ = degrees.
- $M$ = minutes.
- $S$ = seconds.

For south latitude and west longitude, the result is conventionally negative when using signed decimal degrees.

### Example

Convert:

$$
18^\circ30'00''\text{ S}
$$

Calculation:

$$
18+\frac{30}{60}+\frac{0}{3600}=18.5
$$

Because the coordinate is south:

$$
\boxed{-18.5^\circ}
$$

Similarly, 72° 30′ W becomes:

$$
\boxed{-72.5^\circ}
$$

## 5.3 Why coordinates are important

Imagine telling someone:

> The location is approximately 20 km north of the city.

This description is incomplete because the location could be 20 km north in several different directions depending on the starting point and the intended route.

A geographical coordinate pair provides a much more precise reference.

Coordinates are essential for:

- GPS navigation.
- Surveying.
- Mapping.
- Disaster response.
- Search-and-rescue operations.
- Geographical information systems.
- Aviation and maritime navigation.

However, a coordinate pair alone does not guarantee perfect accuracy. The coordinate reference system, datum, measurement quality, and device accuracy also matter.

# 6\. Directions

## 6.1 Definition of direction

**Direction is the orientation of one location or line relative to another, usually expressed with reference to a standard such as north.**

Directions allow a navigator to describe where a destination lies and which way to travel.

There are several ways to express direction:

1. Cardinal directions.
2. Intercardinal directions.
3. Compass points.
4. Compass bearings.
5. Relative directions.
6. Map-grid directions.

Let's examine the main methods.

## 6.2 The four cardinal directions

The four principal or cardinal directions are:

- North (N)
- East (E)
- South (S)
- West (W)

These directions are separated by 90°.

The conventional bearings measured clockwise from north are:

| Direction | Bearing |
| --- | --- |
| North | 000° or 360° |
| East | 090° |
| South | 180° |
| West | 270° |

North is the reference direction.

Turning clockwise from north leads to east, then south, then west, and finally north again.

One complete clockwise rotation equals 360°.

## 6.3 The four intercardinal directions

The intercardinal directions lie halfway between the cardinal directions.

They are:

- Northeast (NE): 045°
- Southeast (SE): 135°
- Southwest (SW): 225°
- Northwest (NW): 315°

Each lies 45° from its neighbouring cardinal directions.

For example, northeast is halfway between north and east.

## 6.4 The eight principal compass points

The eight principal compass points are:

| Compass point | Bearing |
| --- | --- |
| North | 000° |
| Northeast | 045° |
| East | 090° |
| Southeast | 135° |
| South | 180° |
| Southwest | 225° |
| West | 270° |
| Northwest | 315° |

These points are sufficient for many basic navigation tasks.

## 6.5 The sixteen-point compass

The compass can be divided into 16 principal points.

Each adjacent point is separated by:

$$
\frac{360^\circ}{16}=22.5^\circ
$$

Examples include:

- North-northeast (NNE): 022.5°
- East-northeast (ENE): 067.5°
- East-southeast (ESE): 112.5°
- South-southeast (SSE): 157.5°
- South-southwest (SSW): 202.5°
- West-southwest (WSW): 247.5°
- West-northwest (WNW): 292.5°
- North-northwest (NNW): 337.5°

A 16-point compass is useful when directions need to be more precise than the eight principal points provide.

A 32-point compass divides the circle into 11.25° intervals and provides still finer directional descriptions.

## 6.6 True north, magnetic north, and grid north

A critical navigation principle is that **north is not represented by exactly the same direction in every reference system.**

There are three important forms of north.

### A. True north

True north is the direction towards the geographical North Pole along the local meridian.

It is the north reference used by geographical coordinates and true bearings.

True north is also called geographical north.

### B. Magnetic north

Magnetic north refers to the direction indicated by the horizontal component of the Earth's magnetic field.

A magnetic compass aligns itself with the local magnetic field, not directly with the geographical North Pole.

The angular difference between true north and magnetic north is called **magnetic declination**, also known as magnetic variation.

Declination varies with:

- Geographical location.
- Time.
- Changes in the Earth's magnetic field.

It can be easterly or westerly.

### C. Grid north

Grid north is the direction of north represented by the vertical grid lines on a map using a particular grid projection.

Grid north may differ from true north because a flat map is a projection of the Earth's curved surface.

The angular difference between grid north and true north is called **grid convergence**.

The difference depends on the map projection and the location on the map.

## 6.7 Why these differences matter

Suppose a map shows a destination at a true bearing of 060°.

If you use a magnetic compass without accounting for magnetic declination, the direction indicated by the compass may differ from the true bearing.

Likewise, a grid bearing measured on a map may not equal the true bearing.

For accurate navigation, you must know which north reference is being used.

A common error is to measure a grid bearing on a map and then follow the same numerical bearing directly on a magnetic compass.

That can lead to significant errors over long distances.

# 7\. Compass Bearings

## 7.1 Definition of a bearing

**A bearing is the horizontal angle between a reference direction and the direction of a destination or line of travel.**

In conventional whole-circle bearings, the angle is measured clockwise from north.

Bearings are normally expressed in degrees from 000° to 360°.

Examples:

- 000°: North
- 045°: Northeast
- 090°: East
- 135°: Southeast
- 180°: South
- 225°: Southwest
- 270°: West
- 315°: Northwest

The bearing 360° represents the same direction as 000°.

For standard navigation notation, 000° is usually preferred for north.

## 7.2 Types of bearings

Several bearing systems are used in navigation.

### A. True bearing

A true bearing is measured clockwise from true north.

For example:

$$
\boxed{TB=075^\circ}
$$

means the destination lies 75° clockwise from true north.

### B. Magnetic bearing

A magnetic bearing is measured clockwise from magnetic north.

For example:

$$
\boxed{MB=110^\circ}
$$

means the destination lies 110° clockwise from magnetic north.

### C. Grid bearing

A grid bearing is measured clockwise from grid north.

It is particularly useful when plotting routes on a topographic map.

### D. Relative bearing

A relative bearing is measured from the forward direction of a person or vehicle rather than from north.

For example:

- 000° relative: Directly ahead.
- 090° relative: To the right.
- 180° relative: Directly behind.
- 270° relative: To the left.

Relative bearings are especially useful in maritime navigation and for describing the position of objects relative to a moving vehicle.

**Important:** Relative-bearing conventions depend on the context. In the convention used here, angles are measured clockwise from the forward direction.

## 7.3 Whole-circle bearings

Whole-circle bearings use a full circle from 000° to 360°, measured clockwise from north.

This is a convenient format because every horizontal direction can be expressed with a single angle.

Examples:

| Bearing | Approximate direction |
| --- | --- |
| 020° | North-northeast |
| 060° | Northeast sector |
| 090° | East |
| 120° | East-southeast sector |
| 180° | South |
| 240° | West-southwest sector |
| 300° | Northwest sector |
| 350° | North-northwest sector |

The directional names are approximate; the numerical bearing is more precise.

## 7.4 Quadrantal bearings

Quadrantal bearings describe a direction using an angle measured from either north or south towards east or west.

The format is:

$$
\boxed{N\ \theta\ E}
$$

$$
\boxed{S\ \theta\ E}
$$

$$
\boxed{S\ \theta\ W}
$$

$$
\boxed{N\ \theta\ W}
$$

where $\theta$ is between 0° and 90°.

Examples include:

- N 30° E
- S 45° E
- S 20° W
- N 60° W

### Conversion from quadrantal to whole-circle bearings

| Quadrantal bearing | Whole-circle bearing |
| --- | --- |
| N 30° E | 030° |
| S 45° E | 135° |
| S 20° W | 200° |
| N 60° W | 300° |

The conversion rules are:

- N $\theta$ E: Bearing = $\theta$
- S $\theta$ E: Bearing = $180^\circ-\theta$
- S $\theta$ W: Bearing = $180^\circ+\theta$
- N $\theta$ W: Bearing = $360^\circ-\theta$

### Example

Convert N 40° W into a whole-circle bearing.

Using the formula:

$$
360^\circ-40^\circ=320^\circ
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{N40^\circ W=320^\circ}
$$

## 7.5 How to calculate a bearing from a map

Suppose you are at Point A and wish to travel to Point B.

A simple procedure is:

1. Identify Point A.
2. Identify Point B.
3. Draw a straight line from A to B.
4. Identify the correct north reference.
5. Place the centre of a protractor on Point A.
6. Align its north reference with the appropriate north direction.
7. Measure clockwise from north to the line AB.
8. Record the bearing.

If the line from A to B points northeast and makes an angle of 35° clockwise from north, the bearing is:

$$
\boxed{035^\circ}
$$

For a grid map, use grid north when measuring a grid bearing.

If you intend to follow the bearing with a magnetic compass, convert it appropriately using the map's declination information and the required reference system.

## 7.6 Fore bearing and back bearing

The **fore bearing** is the bearing from Point A to Point B.

The **back bearing** is the bearing from Point B to Point A.

They differ by 180°.

For a whole-circle bearing:

$$
\boxed{BB=FB\pm180^\circ}
$$

The correct operation depends on the fore bearing.

- If the fore bearing is less than 180°, add 180°.
- If the fore bearing is greater than or equal to 180°, subtract 180°.

### Example 1

Fore bearing:

$$
FB=065^\circ
$$

Since 065° is less than 180°:

$$
BB=65^\circ+180^\circ
$$

$$
\boxed{BB=245^\circ}
$$

### Example 2

Fore bearing:

$$
FB=230^\circ
$$

Since 230° is greater than 180°:

$$
BB=230^\circ-180^\circ
$$

$$
\boxed{BB=050^\circ}
$$

The fore bearing and back bearing should differ by exactly 180° when both use the same reference direction and are measured at the same time.

In real compass observations, local magnetic interference and measurement errors may cause discrepancies.

## 7.7 Magnetic declination and bearing conversion

Magnetic declination is the angular difference between true north and magnetic north.

To convert a true bearing to a magnetic bearing, use the following convention:

- East declination: Magnetic bearing = True bearing − East declination.
- West declination: Magnetic bearing = True bearing + West declination.

Normalize the result to the range 000°–360°.

This is often remembered as:

**True to magnetic: subtract east, add west.**

For the reverse conversion:

**Magnetic to true: add east, subtract west.**

These rules depend on using the conventional signed declination definition and should always be checked against the compass or map instructions.

### Example: East declination

Suppose:

- True bearing = 080°
- Magnetic declination = 5° E

Then:

$$
MB=080^\circ-5^\circ
$$

$$
\boxed{MB=075^\circ}
$$

### Example: West declination

Suppose:

- True bearing = 120°
- Magnetic declination = 7° W

Then:

$$
MB=120^\circ+7^\circ
$$

$$
\boxed{MB=127^\circ}
$$

These are simplified examples. For real navigation, use the current declination for the location and date, and account for any relevant compass deviation.

## 7.8 Compass deviation

Magnetic declination is caused by the Earth's magnetic field relative to true north.

Compass deviation is a different phenomenon: it is the error caused by magnetic influences near the compass.

Examples include:

- Vehicle metal structures.
- Electrical equipment.
- Magnets.
- Steel objects.
- Certain electronic devices.

In some navigation contexts, especially marine navigation, deviation is treated separately from magnetic variation.

A compass can therefore show an incorrect heading even if you have correctly accounted for the Earth's magnetic declination.

# 8\. The Compass and Its Use

## 8.1 What is a compass?

A compass is a navigation instrument that indicates direction.

A conventional magnetic compass contains a magnetized needle or magnetic element that aligns with the Earth's magnetic field.

The compass normally identifies magnetic north rather than true north.

Other types include gyrocompasses and electronic heading sensors, which operate using different principles.

## 8.2 Main parts of a basic magnetic compass

A typical baseplate compass contains:

- **Magnetic needle:** Aligns with the local magnetic field.
- **Compass housing:** Contains the graduated direction scale.
- **Graduated dial:** Displays bearings from 000° to 360°.
- **Orienting arrow:** Helps align the compass housing with the north reference.
- **Direction-of-travel arrow:** Indicates the direction in which you intend to move.
- **Baseplate:** Helps align the compass with map features.
- **Index line:** Shows the bearing being read.

The precise components vary by compass design.

## 8.3 How to use a compass to follow a bearing

Suppose you need to travel on a magnetic bearing of 060°.

Follow these general steps:

1. Hold the compass level.
2. Rotate the compass housing until 060° aligns with the direction-of-travel arrow or index, according to the compass design.
3. Rotate your body until the magnetic needle aligns with the housing's north indicator.
4. Look along the direction-of-travel arrow.
5. Select a visible landmark in that direction.
6. Travel towards the landmark.
7. Repeat the procedure when you reach it.

Choosing a landmark helps you maintain direction without constantly staring at the compass.

However, if the terrain is dangerous or the route is not visible, you should use a safer and more appropriate navigation technique.

## 8.4 Common compass errors

Compass readings can be affected by:

- Nearby magnets.
- Metal objects.
- Electrical currents.
- Incorrectly holding the compass.
- Confusing true and magnetic bearings.
- Reading the wrong end of the graduated scale.
- Using an outdated magnetic-declination value.
- Confusing the direction of travel with the direction back to the starting point.

To reduce errors:

- Keep the compass away from magnetic and electrical interference.
- Hold it level.
- Read the bearing carefully.
- Confirm the intended direction.
- Check the map's north reference.
- Recheck your position at known landmarks.

# 9\. Distance in Navigation

## 9.1 Definition of distance

Distance is the length of the path travelled between two points.

However, navigation requires us to distinguish between several different types of distance.

### A. Straight-line distance

The straight-line distance is the shortest distance between two points in a flat-plane approximation.

For two points on the Earth's surface, the shortest surface route is generally a geodesic, not a straight line through three-dimensional space.

For nearby points, straight-line distance is often a useful approximation.

### B. Route distance

Route distance is the length of the actual path travelled.

For example, a road between two towns may be 40 km long even though their straight-line separation is only 30 km.

### C. Ground distance

Ground distance is the distance measured along the terrain.

It may differ from the distance measured horizontally on a map, especially in steep terrain.

### D. Map distance

Map distance is the distance measured on a map.

It is converted into ground distance using the map scale.

### E. Horizontal distance

Horizontal distance is the distance between two points measured in a horizontal plane.

Surveying often distinguishes horizontal distance from slope distance.

## 9.2 Units of distance

Common units include:

| Unit | Equivalent |
| --- | --- |
| 1 kilometre | 1,000 metres |
| 1 metre | 100 centimetres |
| 1 mile | 1.609344 kilometres |
| 1 nautical mile | Exactly 1.852 kilometres |
| 1 nautical mile | 1 minute of latitude, approximately |

The nautical mile is defined as exactly 1,852 metres.

It is widely used in maritime and aviation navigation.

A nautical mile is not the same as a statute mile.

$$
1\text{ nautical mile}\approx1.15078\text{ statute miles}
$$

## 9.3 Distance from map scale

A map scale expresses the relationship between a distance on the map and the corresponding distance on the ground.

For example:

$$
1:50,000
$$

means that one unit on the map represents 50,000 of the same units on the ground.

Therefore:

$$
1\text{ cm on the map}=50,000\text{ cm on the ground}
$$

Since:

$$
50,000\text{ cm}=500\text{ m}
$$

A map scale of 1:50,000 means:

$$
\boxed{1\text{ cm on the map}=500\text{ m on the ground}}
$$

### Example 1: Calculating ground distance

A road measures 6 cm on a map with a scale of 1:50,000.

Ground distance:

$$
6\times500=3,000\text{ m}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\text{Ground distance}=3\text{ km}}
$$

### Example 2: Scale of 1:25,000

Suppose a route measures 8 cm on a 1:25,000 map.

One centimetre represents:

$$
25,000\text{ cm}=250\text{ m}
$$

Therefore:

$$
8\times250=2,000\text{ m}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Ground distance}=2\text{ km}}
$$

## 9.4 Measuring irregular routes on maps

A winding road cannot always be measured accurately using a straight ruler.

A useful technique is to measure the route in short sections.

You can use:

- A piece of string.
- A map measurer or opisometer.
- A digital mapping tool.
- A series of short ruler measurements.

Add the section lengths together, then convert the total using the map scale.

For a digital map, ensure that the measurement tool is measuring the intended quantity, such as road distance rather than straight-line distance.

## 9.5 Distance from coordinates

For short distances, coordinate differences can provide approximate distances.

If two points have latitude and longitude differences, a simple local approximation is:

$$
\boxed{N\approx111.1\Delta\phi}
$$

$$
\boxed{E\approx111.3\cos\phi\Delta\lambda}
$$

where:

- $N$ = north–south displacement in kilometres.
- $E$ = east–west displacement in kilometres.
- $\Delta\phi$ = latitude difference in degrees.
- $\Delta\lambda$ = longitude difference in degrees.
- $\phi$ = representative latitude of the two points.

For short distances, the approximate straight-line distance is:

$$
\boxed{D\approx\sqrt{N^2+E^2}}
$$

### Example

Suppose two locations are separated by:

- 3 km north–south.
- 4 km east–west.

Then:

$$
D=\sqrt{3^2+4^2}
$$

$$
D=\sqrt{9+16}
$$

$$
D=\sqrt{25}
$$

$$
\boxed{D=5\text{ km}}
$$

This is the straight-line distance in the local-plane approximation, not necessarily the road distance.

For long distances, polar regions, or high-accuracy applications, use a geodesic calculation on an appropriate Earth model.

# 10\. Speed in Navigation

## 10.1 Definition of speed

**Speed is the distance travelled per unit of time.**

It tells us how quickly a person or vehicle covers a distance.

The basic formula is:

$$
\boxed{S=\frac{D}{T}}
$$

where:

- $S$ = speed.
- $D$ = distance.
- $T$ = time.

The formula can be rearranged:

$$
\boxed{D=S\times T}
$$

and:

$$
\boxed{T=\frac{D}{S}}
$$

These three formulas are among the most important relationships in navigation.

## 10.2 Units of speed

Common units include:

- km/h
- m/s
- mph
- knots

A knot is one nautical mile per hour.

Therefore:

$$
1\text{ knot}=1.852\text{ km/h}
$$

To convert knots to kilometres per hour:

$$
\boxed{\text{km/h}=\text{knots}\times1.852}
$$

To convert kilometres per hour to knots:

$$
\boxed{\text{knots}=\frac{\text{km/h}}{1.852}}
$$

### Example

Convert 20 knots to kilometres per hour.

$$
20\times1.852=37.04
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{20\text{ knots}=37.04\text{ km/h}}
$$

## 10.3 Average speed

Average speed is the total distance travelled divided by the total elapsed time.

$$
\boxed{\text{Average speed}=\frac{\text{Total distance}}{\text{Total time}}}
$$

Average speed is not necessarily the arithmetic mean of the speeds recorded during different parts of a journey.

### Example

A vehicle travels:

- 60 km in the first hour.
- 40 km in the second hour.

Total distance:

$$
60+40=100\text{ km}
$$

Total time:

$$
1+1=2\text{ hours}
$$

Average speed:

$$
\frac{100}{2}=50\text{ km/h}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\text{Average speed}=50\text{ km/h}}
$$

## 10.4 Calculating travel time

Suppose a vehicle must travel 180 km at an average speed of 60 km/h.

Using:

$$
T=\frac{D}{S}
$$

we obtain:

$$
T=\frac{180}{60}
$$

$$
\boxed{T=3\text{ hours}}
$$

This assumes that the average speed over the journey is 60 km/h.

Stops, congestion, terrain, and other delays must be accounted for when estimating the actual arrival time.

## 10.5 Calculating speed from distance and time

Suppose a traveller covers 12 km in 3 hours.

Then:

$$
S=\frac{12}{3}
$$

$$
\boxed{S=4\text{ km/h}}
$$

This is a reasonable example of walking speed, although actual walking speeds vary considerably.

## 10.6 Converting metres per second to kilometres per hour

Since:

$$
1\text{ km}=1,000\text{ m}
$$

and:

$$
1\text{ hour}=3,600\text{ seconds}
$$

we obtain:

$$
1\text{ m/s}=\frac{3,600}{1,000}\text{ km/h}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{1\text{ m/s}=3.6\text{ km/h}}
$$

For example:

$$
5\text{ m/s}\times3.6=18\text{ km/h}
$$

Thus:

$$
\boxed{5\text{ m/s}=18\text{ km/h}}
$$

## 10.7 Speed and navigation accuracy

Speed affects position estimation when a navigator uses elapsed time to estimate distance travelled.

For example, suppose you travel at an estimated speed of 5 km/h for 2 hours.

Estimated distance:

$$
D=5\times2=10\text{ km}
$$

However, if the actual average speed was 4 km/h:

$$
D=4\times2=8\text{ km}
$$

The position estimate would be off by 2 km.

This illustrates an important principle: **an error in estimated speed creates an error in estimated distance, which in turn creates an error in estimated position.**

# 11\. Direction, Distance, and Displacement

These terms are related but are not interchangeable.

## 11.1 Distance

Distance is the total length of the path travelled.

It is a scalar quantity, meaning it has magnitude but no direction.

For example:

A traveller walks 2 km east and then 2 km west.

Total distance:

$$
2+2=4\text{ km}
$$

## 11.2 Displacement

Displacement is the change in position from the starting point to the ending point.

It has both magnitude and direction.

In the previous example, the traveller returns to the starting point.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\text{Displacement}=0}
$$

Even though the total distance travelled is 4 km, the net displacement is zero.

## 11.3 Bearing and displacement

A bearing describes the direction of a line from one point to another.

Displacement combines direction with the straight-line separation between the points.

For example:

A person travels 5 km on a bearing of 090°.

Ignoring terrain and route constraints, the displacement is 5 km east.

If the person instead follows a winding road for 5 km, the final displacement may be much smaller than 5 km.

This distinction is essential when comparing route distance with straight-line movement.

# 12\. Position Fixing

## 12.1 Definition of position fixing

**Position fixing is the process of determining the current geographical location of a person, vehicle, or object using observations, measurements, or positioning systems.**

It answers the question:

> Where exactly am I?

Position fixing is one of the most important aspects of navigation because a navigator must know the starting position before planning a reliable route.

It is also important to determine whether the navigator is still following the intended route.

Position fixing methods include:

1. Landmark identification.
2. Resection using bearings.
3. Intersection of bearings.
4. Dead reckoning.
5. Satellite navigation.
6. Grid references.
7. Surveying techniques.
8. Radio-based positioning.

Each method has strengths and limitations.

## 12.2 Position fixing using landmarks

A landmark is a recognizable feature whose location is known or can be identified on a map.

Examples include:

- A mountain peak.
- A bridge.
- A church tower.
- A road intersection.
- A distinctive building.
- A river junction.
- A radio mast.

A navigator can compare the visible landscape with the map to determine their approximate position.

### Procedure

1. Identify a visible landmark.
2. Find the same landmark on the map.
3. Compare its direction and distance with your surroundings.
4. Identify other visible features.
5. Check whether the features align consistently with your estimated position.
6. Mark the estimated position on the map.

One landmark often provides only a rough position estimate. Multiple well-separated landmarks provide stronger confirmation.

## 12.3 Position fixing by intersection of bearings

This method uses bearings observed from known locations or to known landmarks, depending on the particular surveying or navigation arrangement.

The basic geometric principle is that lines of known direction intersect at a point.

For example, suppose two known landmarks are visible from an unknown position.

If you measure the bearing from your position to each landmark, you can draw the corresponding lines backwards from the landmarks on the map.

The intersection of those lines estimates your position.

This method is commonly called **resection** when determining an unknown position from observations to known points.

### Example

Suppose you can identify two landmarks on a map:

- Landmark A.
- Landmark B.

You measure their bearings from your current position.

You then:

1. Locate A and B on the map.
2. Convert the observed bearings to the correct map reference system.
3. Draw a line from A in the reciprocal direction of its observed bearing.
4. Draw a line from B in the reciprocal direction of its observed bearing.
5. Identify the intersection of the two lines.

The intersection estimates your position.

In practice, small measurement errors may cause the lines to cross at slightly different places. A third landmark can provide a useful check.

**Important distinction:** If bearings are measured from a known station towards an unknown point, the lines can be intersected directly. If bearings are measured from the unknown position towards known landmarks, the reciprocal bearings must be plotted.

## 12.4 Position fixing by three-point resection

Three-point resection uses observations to three known landmarks to estimate an unknown position.

It provides an additional check against errors that may occur when using only two landmarks.

The procedure is broadly similar to two-point resection, but all three observations must be consistent with the same position.

If the three lines do not meet at exactly one point, the discrepancy may be caused by:

- Compass errors.
- Poor landmark identification.
- Incorrect map positions.
- Magnetic interference.
- Measurement inaccuracies.

A navigator can use the pattern of disagreement to investigate possible errors.

## 12.5 Position fixing by satellite navigation

Global Navigation Satellite Systems (GNSS) determine position using signals transmitted by satellites.

Examples include:

- GPS.
- Galileo.
- GLONASS.
- BeiDou.

A GNSS receiver estimates its position by processing signals from multiple satellites.

The system can provide:

- Latitude.
- Longitude.
- Altitude or height.
- Speed over ground.
- Course over ground.
- Time.

The receiver estimates its distance from satellites using the travel time of radio signals and combines these measurements to determine its position.

A conventional three-dimensional position solution generally requires measurements from at least four satellites because the receiver must solve for three spatial coordinates and its clock offset.

More satellites can improve the reliability and geometry of the solution.

However, satellite positioning can be affected by:

- Buildings.
- Dense forests.
- Mountains.
- Signal blockage.
- Reflections from nearby surfaces.
- Atmospheric effects.
- Satellite geometry.
- Interference or deliberate jamming.

GNSS is powerful, but it should not automatically be assumed to be error-free.

## 12.6 Position fixing using grid references

A map grid divides a map into a network of squares.

A grid reference identifies a location using coordinates relative to the grid's axes.

Grid references are widely used with topographic maps.

For many metric map grids, eastings increase towards the east and northings increase towards the north.

The conventional reading order is:

$$
\boxed{\text{Eastings first, northings second}}
$$

A common mnemonic is:

**Along the corridor, then up the stairs.**

This means:

1. Read the easting.
2. Read the northing.

For example, a six-digit grid reference might be written as:

$$
123456
$$

This could be divided into:

- Easting: 123.
- Northing: 456.

The actual interpretation depends on the particular grid system and its reference origin.

A grid reference must always be interpreted using the correct map and grid specification.

## 12.7 Position fixing by dead reckoning

Dead reckoning estimates a current position from a known starting position using the direction travelled, distance travelled, and elapsed time.

The basic principle is:

$$
\boxed{\text{New position}=\text{Starting position}+\text{Estimated displacement}}
$$

Suppose you begin at a known location and travel 10 km due east.

If the direction and distance are known accurately, your estimated position is 10 km east of the starting point.

However, dead reckoning errors accumulate over time.

For example:

- The compass may have an error of 3°.
- The estimated speed may be inaccurate.
- The route may curve around obstacles.
- The terrain may force deviations.
- The starting position may be uncertain.

Therefore, dead reckoning should be checked against landmarks, GPS, or another independent method whenever possible.

## 12.8 Position fixing using map contours

Contour lines connect points of equal elevation.

They help navigators identify terrain features and compare the map with the surrounding landscape.

Contour information can assist with position fixing by identifying:

- Ridges.
- Valleys.
- Saddles.
- Hilltops.
- Steep slopes.
- Drainage patterns.

For example, if a traveller knows that they are on a ridge between two valleys, the contour pattern may narrow down their position considerably.

Contour-based navigation is particularly useful in mountainous or remote terrain where roads and other landmarks may be absent.

# 13\. Dead Reckoning and Estimated Position

Position fixing and dead reckoning are related, but they are not identical.

A position fix is based on observations or measurements that establish the current position.

Dead reckoning estimates the current position from a previously known position and subsequent movement.

This distinction is important.

## 13.1 Worked dead-reckoning example

Suppose a vehicle starts at a known point and travels:

- 10 km east.
- Then 6 km north.

Ignoring roads and terrain, the final displacement can be calculated using the Pythagorean theorem.

$$
D=\sqrt{10^2+6^2}
$$

$$
D=\sqrt{100+36}
$$

$$
D=\sqrt{136}
$$

$$
\boxed{D\approx11.66\text{ km}}
$$

The final point lies northeast of the starting point.

To calculate the bearing, use:

$$
\theta=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{E}{N}\right)
$$

Here:

- East displacement $E=10$ km.
- North displacement $N=6$ km.

Because a whole-circle bearing is measured clockwise from north:

$$
\theta=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{10}{6}\right)
$$

$$
\theta\approx59.04^\circ
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\text{Bearing}\approx059^\circ}
$$

The estimated final position is approximately 11.66 km from the starting point on a bearing of 059°.

This calculation assumes a flat local coordinate plane and ignores terrain, road curvature, and changes in geographical coordinates over the journey.

## 13.2 Estimated position versus confirmed position

An estimated position may be based on:

- Distance travelled.
- Direction travelled.
- Speed and time.
- Previous position information.

A confirmed position is supported by a reliable observation or positioning method, such as:

- A clear map-matched landmark.
- A verified GNSS fix.
- A survey observation.
- A reliable known control point.

In practice, navigators often combine both methods.

They estimate their position continuously and periodically confirm it using independent observations.

# 14\. Basic Navigation Principles

Navigation becomes much more reliable when a navigator follows a systematic method rather than simply choosing a direction and continuing.

## Principle 1: Know your starting position

Before beginning a journey, identify your current position as accurately as practical.

This may involve:

- Reading GPS coordinates.
- Identifying your location on a map.
- Using a known landmark.
- Obtaining a grid reference.

If your starting position is wrong, every subsequent calculation may also be wrong.

## Principle 2: Identify the destination

The destination must be clearly defined.

It could be:

- A geographical coordinate.
- A road intersection.
- A mountain summit.
- A building.
- A trail junction.
- A grid reference.

A vague destination makes route planning and progress monitoring difficult.

## Principle 3: Select a suitable route

The shortest route is not always the best route.

A practical route may need to account for:

- Terrain.
- Road conditions.
- Rivers.
- Mountains.
- Buildings.
- Restricted areas.
- Weather.
- Visibility.
- Available supplies.
- Safety considerations.

A route that is geometrically short may be physically difficult or dangerous.

## Principle 4: Determine the required direction

Calculate or identify the direction from the starting position to the next waypoint.

Express it as a bearing when greater precision is required.

For example:

$$
\text{Bearing}=045^\circ
$$

means northeast.

However, the bearing must be measured from the correct reference north.

## Principle 5: Estimate the distance

Determine the distance to the next waypoint.

Possible methods include:

- Measuring the route on a map.
- Reading a digital map.
- Calculating distance from coordinates.
- Using a vehicle's distance measurement.
- Estimating distance from pace count or elapsed time.

The method should match the required level of accuracy.

## Principle 6: Estimate the travel time

Use:

$$
\boxed{T=\frac{D}{S}}
$$

For example, a 12 km route at an average speed of 4 km/h takes:

$$
T=\frac{12}{4}=3\text{ hours}
$$

This estimate should account for expected stops and terrain when appropriate.

## Principle 7: Follow the planned route

Maintain the intended direction while monitoring your surroundings.

Depending on the situation, this may involve:

- Using a compass.
- Following a road.
- Following a marked trail.
- Tracking GPS coordinates.
- Observing landmarks.
- Following contour features.

Do not assume that maintaining one compass bearing will always lead to the destination. Terrain and route constraints may require intermediate waypoints.

## Principle 8: Monitor progress

Compare the expected position with the actual position at regular intervals.

For example, if you expect to reach a road junction after 2 km, check whether you have reached it.

If the landmark does not appear when expected, consider whether:

- The distance estimate was wrong.
- The bearing was incorrect.
- The starting position was inaccurate.
- The route has changed.
- Visibility is poor.

## Principle 9: Fix your position periodically

A navigator should not rely indefinitely on an initial position estimate.

Use independent position checks whenever possible.

This prevents small errors from accumulating into major deviations.

## Principle 10: Correct deviations early

If you discover that you are off course, stop and determine your actual position before making a correction.

Do not blindly turn towards the destination without understanding where you are.

A correction based on an incorrect position can make the situation worse.

## Principle 11: Understand navigation limitations

Every navigation method has limitations.

A magnetic compass may be affected by nearby metal.

A GPS receiver may lose satellite visibility.

A map may be outdated.

A road may be closed.

A dead-reckoning estimate may accumulate errors.

Reliable navigation therefore involves checking information from more than one source when the consequences of error are significant.

# 15\. Map Reading and Map Orientation

A map is one of the most important tools in terrestrial navigation.

It represents geographical features on a flat surface.

Maps may show:

- Roads.
- Rivers.
- Buildings.
- Trails.
- Elevation.
- Boundaries.
- Vegetation.
- Grid lines.
- Geographical coordinates.

## 15.1 Map orientation

Map orientation means aligning the map with the corresponding directions on the ground.

On many conventional topographic maps, grid north is towards the top of the page.

However, the map's north reference and the actual direction of true north may differ.

To orient a map using a compass:

1. Identify the map's north reference.
2. Check whether the map uses true north or grid north.
3. Determine the relevant declination or grid-convergence information.
4. Adjust the compass or map orientation appropriately.
5. Compare the map with visible terrain features.

Always follow the specific instructions for the map and compass being used.

## 15.2 Map symbols

Maps use symbols to represent real-world features.

Examples include:

- Blue lines for many rivers and streams.
- Contour lines for elevation.
- Symbols for roads and tracks.
- Symbols for buildings and settlements.
- Markings for bridges.
- Symbols for vegetation and land cover.

The exact colours and symbols depend on the map's legend.

A navigator should always consult the legend before interpreting unfamiliar symbols.

## 15.3 Contour lines

Contour lines connect locations with equal elevation.

If a contour line is labelled 500 m, all points on that line have an elevation of approximately 500 m above the map's specified vertical reference.

The vertical difference between successive contour lines is called the **contour interval**.

For example, if contours are labelled:

- 100 m
- 120 m
- 140 m

then the contour interval is:

$$
\boxed{20\text{ m}}
$$

Contour lines can reveal terrain shape.

### General contour interpretation

- Closely spaced contours indicate a steep slope.
- Widely spaced contours indicate a gentle slope.
- Closed contours often indicate a hill or depression, depending on the contour markings.
- V-shaped contours crossing streams often point upstream.
- Contours crossing a ridge or valley form characteristic patterns.

These rules help navigators predict terrain before moving into it.

# 16\. Grid References and Coordinate Systems

Latitude and longitude form a global coordinate system, but many topographic maps use projected grid systems.

## 16.1 Why use a grid?

A rectangular grid can make local navigation and measurement more convenient than working directly with angular coordinates.

Grid systems are widely used for:

- Military navigation.
- Land surveying.
- Topographic mapping.
- Engineering.
- Geographic information systems.

The grid's coordinates generally use linear units, such as metres.

## 16.2 Eastings and northings

In many projected grid systems:

- **Easting** measures position towards the east.
- **Northing** measures position towards the north.

A grid coordinate may be written as:

$$
E=450,000\text{ m}
$$

$$
N=2,150,000\text{ m}
$$

These values are meaningful only when the grid system and its coordinate reference system are known.

Different grids use different origins, projections, and coordinate conventions.

## 16.3 Latitude and longitude versus grid coordinates

| Feature | Latitude and longitude | Grid coordinates |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Coordinate type | Angular | Usually linear |
| Typical units | Degrees, minutes, seconds | Metres or feet |
| Reference system | Global geographical framework | Defined projected grid |
| Main use | Global positioning | Local and regional mapping |
| Geometry | Curved geographical surface | Flat projected coordinate plane |
| Distance calculation | Requires geographical calculations | Often straightforward locally |

A grid coordinate is not automatically interchangeable with latitude and longitude.

A conversion requires the correct coordinate reference system.

# 17\. Navigation Calculations Using Bearings

Bearings can be combined with distances to calculate changes in position.

For short distances over relatively flat terrain, the east–west and north–south components of movement can be calculated using trigonometry.

Assume:

- Bearings are measured clockwise from north.
- $D$ is the distance travelled.
- $E$ is the eastward displacement.
- $N$ is the northward displacement.
- $\theta$ is the bearing.

Then:

$$
\boxed{E=D\sin\theta}
$$

$$
\boxed{N=D\cos\theta}
$$

These formulas use a bearing measured clockwise from north.

The signs of the results determine whether the displacement is towards the north, south, east, or west.

For example:

- Positive $E$: east.
- Negative $E$: west.
- Positive $N$: north.
- Negative $N$: south.

## Worked Example 1: Travel on a bearing of 060°

A traveller moves 10 km on a bearing of 060°.

Calculate the eastward and northward components.

Eastward displacement:

$$
E=10\sin60^\circ
$$

Since:

$$
\sin60^\circ\approx0.8660
$$

$$
E\approx10\times0.8660
$$

$$
\boxed{E\approx8.66\text{ km}}
$$

Northward displacement:

$$
N=10\cos60^\circ
$$

Since:

$$
\cos60^\circ=0.5
$$

$$
N=10\times0.5
$$

$$
\boxed{N=5\text{ km}}
$$

Therefore, the traveller moves approximately:

- 8.66 km east.
- 5 km north.

The total displacement remains 10 km.

## Worked Example 2: Travel on a bearing of 210°

A vehicle travels 20 km on a bearing of 210°.

Calculate its east–west and north–south displacement components.

Eastward component:

$$
E=20\sin210^\circ
$$

Since:

$$
\sin210^\circ=-0.5
$$

$$
E=20(-0.5)
$$

$$
\boxed{E=-10\text{ km}}
$$

The negative sign means 10 km west.

Northward component:

$$
N=20\cos210^\circ
$$

Since:

$$
\cos210^\circ\approx-0.8660
$$

$$
N\approx20(-0.8660)
$$

$$
\boxed{N\approx-17.32\text{ km}}
$$

The negative sign means 17.32 km south.

Therefore, the vehicle moves approximately:

- 10 km west.
- 17.32 km south.

These components are useful for estimating a new position on a local grid.

# 18\. Navigation Using Multiple Legs

A journey often consists of several separate legs.

Each leg may have its own:

- Bearing.
- Distance.
- Speed.
- Travel time.

To calculate the final position, determine the displacement components for every leg and add them together.

## Worked Example

A traveller follows this route:

- Leg 1: 8 km east.
- Leg 2: 6 km north.
- Leg 3: 2 km west.

### Step 1: Calculate the total east–west displacement

Eastward movement:

$$
8\text{ km}
$$

Westward movement:

$$
2\text{ km}
$$

Net eastward displacement:

$$
8-2=6\text{ km}
$$

### Step 2: Calculate the total north–south displacement

Northward movement:

$$
6\text{ km}
$$

No southward movement occurs.

Net northward displacement:

$$
6\text{ km}
$$

### Step 3: Calculate the final displacement

$$
D=\sqrt{6^2+6^2}
$$

$$
D=\sqrt{36+36}
$$

$$
D=\sqrt{72}
$$

$$
\boxed{D\approx8.49\text{ km}}
$$

### Step 4: Calculate the bearing

The eastward and northward displacements are equal.

Therefore:

$$
\theta=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{6}{6}\right)
$$

$$
\theta=45^\circ
$$

Thus:

$$
\boxed{\text{Final bearing}=045^\circ}
$$

The final position is approximately 8.49 km northeast of the starting point.

However, the total distance travelled is:

$$
8+6+2=16\text{ km}
$$

This demonstrates the difference between total distance and net displacement.

# 19\. Position Accuracy and Navigation Errors

No navigation method is perfectly accurate.

A good navigator must understand how errors arise and how to reduce their effects.

## 19.1 Direction errors

A small error in bearing can create a substantial lateral displacement over a long distance.

Suppose a traveller intends to travel 10 km north but follows a bearing 5° east of north.

The approximate eastward error is:

$$
E=10\sin5^\circ
$$

Since:

$$
\sin5^\circ\approx0.0872
$$

$$
E\approx10\times0.0872
$$

$$
\boxed{E\approx0.87\text{ km}}
$$

A bearing error of only 5° produces an eastward deviation of approximately 870 m after travelling 10 km.

This is why accurate bearing measurement and periodic position fixing are important.

## 19.2 Distance errors

Distance estimates may be inaccurate because of:

- Incorrect map-scale interpretation.
- Poor measurement.
- Changes in terrain.
- Incorrect pace counting.
- Vehicle odometer error.
- Misjudging the distance to a landmark.

If the estimated distance is 10 km but the actual distance is 12 km, the position estimate may be significantly wrong.

## 19.3 Time errors

Dead reckoning often uses speed and elapsed time to estimate distance.

If the elapsed time is incorrect or the average speed changes unexpectedly, the estimated position will also be incorrect.

## 19.4 Map errors

Maps may be affected by:

- Outdated roads.
- Incomplete geographical information.
- Inaccurate features.
- Incorrectly interpreted symbols.
- Projection distortion.
- Datum mismatches.

A map should be appropriate for the intended task and area.

## 19.5 GPS errors

GNSS accuracy may be reduced by:

- Satellite geometry.
- Signal blockage.
- Reflected signals.
- Atmospheric delays.
- Poor receiver performance.
- Interference.
- Incorrect coordinate reference settings.

A GPS coordinate may look precise because it contains many decimal places, but numerical precision does not guarantee positional accuracy.

## 19.6 Error reduction

To reduce navigation errors:

1. Confirm the starting position.
2. Use a suitable map.
3. Check the map scale.
4. Distinguish true north, magnetic north, and grid north.
5. Use the correct magnetic-declination information.
6. Maintain a record of bearings and distances.
7. Check the route at known landmarks.
8. Recalculate the estimated position when necessary.
9. Use independent methods to verify important positions.
10. Avoid continuing blindly when the observed terrain disagrees with the map.

# 20\. Practical Navigation Example: From Start to Destination

Consider a simple terrestrial navigation problem.

A traveller begins at Point A and must reach Point B.

The following information is available:

- Straight-line distance: 12 km.
- Bearing from A to B: 060°.
- Average speed: 4 km/h.
- The terrain is assumed to permit direct travel for the purposes of this example.

We will determine the direction, displacement components, and estimated travel time.

## Step 1: Determine the direction

The bearing is:

$$
060^\circ
$$

This means 60° clockwise from north.

The direction is northeast.

More precisely, it is 30° north of east.

## Step 2: Calculate the eastward displacement

$$
E=D\sin\theta
$$

$$
E=12\sin60^\circ
$$

$$
E\approx12\times0.8660
$$

$$
\boxed{E\approx10.39\text{ km}}
$$

## Step 3: Calculate the northward displacement

$$
N=D\cos\theta
$$

$$
N=12\cos60^\circ
$$

$$
N=12\times0.5
$$

$$
\boxed{N=6\text{ km}}
$$

The destination lies approximately 10.39 km east and 6 km north of the starting point.

## Step 4: Calculate travel time

$$
T=\frac{D}{S}
$$

$$
T=\frac{12}{4}
$$

$$
\boxed{T=3\text{ hours}}
$$

The estimated travel time is 3 hours.

This excludes breaks and any delays caused by obstacles or changes in terrain.

## Step 5: Plan position checks

A practical navigator would divide the route into manageable sections.

For example:

- First checkpoint: A visible landmark after approximately 3 km.
- Second checkpoint: A trail junction after approximately 6 km.
- Third checkpoint: A prominent geographical feature after approximately 9 km.
- Final checkpoint: Point B.

The exact checkpoints would depend on the map and terrain.

At each checkpoint, the traveller should confirm the position and update the remaining distance.

This is more reliable than travelling for three hours without checking progress.

# 22\. Essential Navigation Formulas

These are the formulas you should know for basic terrestrial navigation.

### Latitude and longitude

Approximate north–south distance:

$$
\boxed{D\approx111\Delta\phi\text{ km}}
$$

where $\Delta\phi$ is the latitude difference in degrees.

Approximate east–west distance:

$$
\boxed{D\approx111.32\cos\phi\,\Delta\lambda\text{ km}}
$$

where:

- $\phi$ = representative latitude.
- $\Delta\lambda$ = longitude difference in degrees.

### Distance, speed, and time

$$
\boxed{S=\frac{D}{T}}
$$

$$
\boxed{D=S\times T}
$$

$$
\boxed{T=\frac{D}{S}}
$$

### Map scale

For a representative fraction scale of $1:n$:

$$
\boxed{\text{Ground distance}=\text{Map distance}\times n}
$$

Both distances must initially use the same units.

### Bearings

Back bearing:

$$
\boxed{BB=FB\pm180^\circ}
$$

True to magnetic:

$$
\boxed{MB=TB-\delta}
$$

where $\delta$ is signed magnetic declination, defined as positive for east and negative for west.

Normalize the result to 000°–360°.

### Displacement components

For distance $D$ travelled on a whole-circle bearing $\theta$:

$$
\boxed{E=D\sin\theta}
$$

$$
\boxed{N=D\cos\theta}
$$

### Resultant displacement

If the eastward and northward components are known:

$$
\boxed{D=\sqrt{E^2+N^2}}
$$

For a bearing measured clockwise from north:

$$
\boxed{\theta=\operatorname{atan2}(E,N)}
$$

Convert the result into the range 000°–360°.

Using a two-argument arctangent function avoids the quadrant errors that can occur when using a simple inverse tangent.

### Unit conversions

$$
\boxed{1\text{ nautical mile}=1.852\text{ km}}
$$

$$
\boxed{1\text{ knot}=1.852\text{ km/h}}
$$

$$
\boxed{1\text{ m/s}=3.6\text{ km/h}}
$$

$$
\boxed{1^\circ=60'}
$$

$$
\boxed{1'=60''}
$$

# 25\. Final Summary: How All the Concepts Fit Together

Terrestrial navigation is not a collection of unrelated formulas. It is a coordinated process in which geographical position, direction, distance, speed, time, and observations work together.

Consider the complete sequence.

**First, determine your position.**

Use latitude and longitude, a grid reference, a map, landmarks, or GNSS.

**Second, identify your destination.**

Specify the destination using coordinates, a grid reference, or a recognizable geographical feature.

**Third, determine the direction.**

Calculate a bearing and establish whether it refers to true north, magnetic north, or grid north.

**Fourth, calculate the distance.**

Measure the route on a map, obtain it from a digital navigation system, or estimate it from geographical coordinates.

**Fifth, calculate the expected travel time.**

Use:

$$
T=\frac{D}{S}
$$

**Sixth, travel along the planned route.**

Follow the appropriate bearing, road, trail, or sequence of waypoints.

**Seventh, monitor your progress.**

Compare your expected position with visible landmarks, map features, or positioning-system information.

**Finally, confirm your position and correct deviations.**

Use reliable observations to update your position and adjust the route when necessary.

The most important principle is this:

> **Good navigation requires knowing where you are, knowing where you want to go, understanding the direction and distance between those points, and repeatedly checking that your actual position agrees with your plan.**

Latitude and longitude establish geographical position. Directions and bearings describe orientation. Distance and speed help estimate movement and travel time. Position fixing confirms location. Maps, compasses, and satellite navigation provide complementary information.

When these elements are understood and used together, they provide the foundation for accurate, systematic, and dependable terrestrial navigation.
